# Domain 27: Cybersecurity Human Factors, Security Culture and Social Engineering

## 1. Domain Overview

Cybersecurity Human Factors, Security Culture and Social Engineering focuses on the human, behavioral, organizational, and social dimensions of cybersecurity.

Technology alone cannot provide complete cybersecurity protection.

People interact with:

- Systems
- Applications
- Devices
- Identity systems
- Data
- Physical environments
- Security controls
- Business processes
- External parties

Human behavior can therefore influence both security exposure and resilience.

This domain connects:

Human Factors → Behavior → Security Culture → Social Engineering → Human-Centered Controls → Organizational Resilience

---

## 2. Purpose

The purpose of this domain is to understand how human behavior and organizational culture influence cybersecurity risk.

Key objectives include:

- Understand human factors in cybersecurity
- Understand security culture
- Understand social engineering
- Understand phishing and related attacks
- Understand insider risk
- Improve security awareness
- Design effective security behavior programs
- Apply human-centered security principles
- Reduce avoidable human error
- Improve security communication
- Integrate people into security architecture
- Strengthen organizational resilience

---

## 3. Human Factors in Cybersecurity

Human factors refer to characteristics of people, tasks, technology, environments, and organizational conditions that influence behavior and performance.

Cybersecurity human factors can include:

- Attention
- Workload
- Usability
- Training
- Decision-making
- Communication
- Organizational processes
- Incentives
- Fatigue
- Time pressure
- Complexity
- Trust
- Context

Security controls should consider how people actually work rather than assuming perfect human behavior.

---

## 4. Human-Centered Security

Human-centered security designs security controls around legitimate user needs, behaviors, capabilities, and constraints.

It considers:

- User experience
- Accessibility
- Usability
- Security requirements
- Workflow
- Cognitive load
- Error conditions
- Recovery
- Feedback

The objective is to make secure behavior practical and understandable.

---

## 5. Usable Security

Usable security aims to make security controls understandable and practical without unnecessarily preventing legitimate work.

Examples include:

- Clear authentication flows
- Understandable security warnings
- Effective passwordless authentication
- Simple reporting mechanisms
- Clear access requests
- Understandable privacy notices
- Recoverable security processes

Poor usability can lead users to develop workarounds that weaken security.

---

## 6. Security Culture

Security culture represents the shared attitudes, behaviors, expectations, and practices relating to security within an organization.

A positive security culture can include:

- Leadership support
- Clear accountability
- Appropriate training
- Easy incident reporting
- Secure defaults
- Open communication
- Continuous learning
- Consistent security practices

Security culture should be treated as an organizational capability rather than only an awareness campaign.

---

## 7. Security Awareness

Security awareness helps people recognize security risks and understand their responsibilities.

Awareness topics may include:

- Phishing
- Password and authentication security
- Data protection
- Device security
- Physical security
- Social engineering
- Incident reporting
- Remote work security
- Cloud application security
- Mobile security

Awareness should be relevant to the individual's role and environment.

---

## 8. Security Training

Security training develops practical knowledge and skills.

Examples include:

- General security training
- Role-based training
- Secure development training
- Administrator training
- Incident response training
- Executive awareness
- OT security training
- Privacy training
- Social engineering awareness

Training should be reinforced through practical exercises and continuous learning.

### Administrators

### Security Professionals

- Design controls

## 10. Social Engineering

Social engineering involves manipulating people into taking actions or revealing information that may compromise security.

Techniques can exploit:

- Trust
- Authority
- Urgency
- Fear
- Curiosity
- Familiarity
- Reciprocity
- Social pressure

Social engineering can target employees, customers, administrators, executives, suppliers, and support personnel.

---

## 11. Phishing

Phishing uses deceptive communications to influence recipients into performing an action or disclosing information.

Common channels include:

- Email
- Messaging platforms
- Collaboration tools
- Social media
- SMS
- Voice calls
- Web pages

Potential objectives include:

- Credential theft
- Malware delivery
- Fraud
- Financial theft
- Unauthorized access
- Information gathering

---

## 12. Spear Phishing

Spear phishing is targeted phishing directed at a specific person or organization.

Attackers may use:

- Organizational information
- Job roles
- Business relationships
- Public information
- Previous communications
- Impersonation

Targeted attacks can appear more credible because they contain context relevant to the recipient.

---

## 13. Business Email Compromise

Business Email Compromise (BEC) involves using compromised or deceptive email identities to influence business activity.

Potential objectives include:

- Fraudulent payments
- Invoice manipulation
- Credential theft
- Sensitive information disclosure
- Unauthorized transactions

Controls can include:

- Strong authentication
- Payment verification
- Segregation of duties
- Out-of-band verification
- Email security
- Staff awareness

---

## 14. Vishing

Vishing is voice-based social engineering.

Attackers may impersonate:

- Bank representatives
- IT support
- Executives
- Vendors
- Government personnel
- Service providers

Verification procedures should not rely solely on caller identity or caller-provided information.

---

## 15. Smishing

Smishing is social engineering delivered through SMS or messaging services.

Examples include messages pretending to be:

- Banks
- Delivery companies
- Employers
- Government services
- Financial institutions
- Technology providers

Users should independently verify unexpected requests before taking action.

---

## 16. Pretexting

Pretexting involves creating a fabricated scenario to persuade someone to provide information or perform an action.

Examples include impersonating:

- Technical support
- Managers
- Vendors
- Auditors
- Customers
- Service providers

Pretexting defenses include identity verification and well-defined authorization procedures.

---

## 17. Impersonation

Impersonation attempts to make a malicious actor appear to be a trusted individual or organization.

Targets may include:

- Executives
- Employees
- IT administrators
- Vendors
- Customers
- Partners

Controls should include independent verification and least-privilege access.

---

## 18. Baiting

Baiting uses an attractive or tempting offer to influence a target.

Examples may include:

- Suspicious downloads
- Fake software
- Malicious media
- Fraudulent rewards
- Unexpected files

Security awareness should teach users to evaluate unexpected offers and files carefully.

---

## 19. Quid Pro Quo

Quid pro quo social engineering offers an apparent benefit in exchange for information or action.

Examples include:

- Fake technical support
- Fake surveys
- Fake rewards
- Fraudulent service offers

Users should verify the legitimacy of the request and provider.

---

## 20. Tailgating and Physical Social Engineering

Physical social engineering manipulates people to gain physical access.

Examples include:

- Tailgating
- Impersonation
- Fake maintenance personnel
- Unauthorized visitor access
- Badge misuse

Physical security is therefore part of cybersecurity defense.

---

## 21. Social Engineering Attack Lifecycle

A generalized social engineering lifecycle can include:

1. Target identification
2. Information gathering
3. Trust establishment
4. Pretext creation
5. Engagement
6. Manipulation
7. Requested action
8. Exploitation
9. Follow-up
10. Monetization or further compromise

Understanding the lifecycle helps organizations design layered defenses.

---

## 22. Human Attack Surface

The human attack surface may include:

- Employees
- Contractors
- Administrators
- Executives
- Developers
- Support staff
- Vendors
- Customers
- Partners
- Temporary workers

Security programs should identify which human interactions can affect critical assets and processes.

---

## 23. Insider Risk

Insider risk refers to security risk associated with individuals who have authorized access to organizational systems, facilities, or information.

Potential insider-risk categories can include:

- Malicious insiders
- Negligent insiders
- Compromised insiders
- Misuse of legitimate access

Insider-risk programs should respect privacy, employment law, organizational policy, and proportionality.

---

## 24. Malicious Insider Risk

A malicious insider intentionally misuses authorized access.

Potential activities include:

- Data theft
- Fraud
- Sabotage
- Unauthorized disclosure
- Privilege abuse

Controls may include:

- Least privilege
- Segregation of duties
- Monitoring
- Access reviews
- Data protection
- Strong offboarding

---

## 25. Negligent Insider Risk

Negligent behavior can create security exposure without malicious intent.

Examples include:

- Sending information to the wrong recipient
- Misconfiguring systems
- Using unauthorized applications
- Mishandling sensitive information
- Falling for phishing
- Leaving devices unsecured

Security programs should address systemic causes rather than relying only on punishment.

---

## 26. Compromised User Accounts

An attacker may compromise a legitimate user's account and use it to perform malicious activity.

Potential controls include:

- Strong authentication
- Phishing-resistant authentication
- Conditional access
- Session monitoring
- Anomaly detection
- Least privilege
- Rapid credential revocation

---

## 27. Privileged Human Risk

Privileged users can have significant access to systems and data.

Examples include:

- System administrators
- Cloud administrators
- Database administrators
- Security administrators
- OT engineers

Controls can include:

- Privileged access management
- Just-in-time access
- Least privilege
- Strong authentication
- Session monitoring
- Separation of duties

---

## 28. Security Behavior

Security behavior describes how people actually interact with security controls and processes.

Examples include:

- Password practices
- MFA usage
- Phishing reporting
- Software installation
- Data handling
- Incident reporting
- Device protection

Behavior should be measured carefully and interpreted in context.

---

## 29. Human Error

Human error can result from:

- Ambiguous processes
- Poor usability
- Excessive workload
- Lack of training
- Time pressure
- Complex systems
- Miscommunication
- Fatigue
- Inadequate safeguards

Security engineering should attempt to reduce the consequences of predictable errors.

---

## 30. Security by Design for Human Behavior

Security controls should account for realistic human behavior.

Design approaches include:

- Secure defaults
- Clear warnings
- Least privilege
- Strong authentication
- Confirmation for high-impact actions
- Reversible actions where possible
- Error prevention
- Simple reporting
- Recovery mechanisms

The objective is to make the secure path practical.

---

## 31. Security Friction

Security controls can introduce friction into business workflows.

Examples include:

- Authentication steps
- Approval processes
- Access restrictions
- Security prompts
- Device controls

Excessive friction may encourage workarounds.

The goal is appropriate security friction based on risk and context.

---

## 32. Authentication and Human Factors

Authentication systems should consider:

- Usability
- Accessibility
- Recovery
- Device availability
- User context
- Phishing resistance
- Administrative burden

Modern authentication approaches may include:

- Passkeys
- Hardware security keys
- Biometrics
- MFA
- Risk-based authentication

Security requirements and usability should be considered together.

---

## 33. Security Warnings

Effective security warnings should be:

- Clear
- Specific
- Timely
- Understandable
- Actionable

Warnings that are excessive, vague, or repetitive may become less effective.

---

## 34. Security Nudges

Security nudges provide timely prompts that encourage safer behavior.

Examples include:

- MFA reminders
- Data classification prompts
- External-recipient warnings
- Suspicious-link warnings
- Secure configuration reminders

Nudges should support rather than replace technical controls.

---

A responsible program should consider:

- Clear objectives
- Appropriate authorization
- Privacy
- Legal requirements
- Psychological safety
- Measurement methodology
- Educational follow-up

The objective should be improving resilience rather than humiliating individuals.

## 40. Risk Perception

People may perceive cybersecurity risks differently based on:

- Experience
- Role
- Information available
- Familiarity
- Organizational culture
- Perceived consequences
- Trust

Security communication should address relevant context rather than relying only on fear-based messaging.

## 41. Cognitive Biases in Security

Human decision-making can be influenced by biases such as:

- Confirmation bias
- Availability bias
- Anchoring
- Authority bias
- Normalcy bias
- Overconfidence

Security professionals should use structured processes and independent verification to reduce avoidable decision errors.

---

## 42. Authority and Trust

Social engineering often attempts to exploit perceived authority.

Examples include impersonating:

- Executives
- Managers
- Security personnel
- Government officials
- Vendors

Security procedures should allow legitimate requests to be independently verified.

---

## 43. Urgency and Fear

Attackers may create artificial urgency:

- "Act immediately"
- "Your account will be disabled"
- "Payment is overdue"
- "Your system is compromised"

Security awareness should teach users to pause and independently verify high-pressure requests.

---

## 44. Security Reporting Culture

Employees should have accessible mechanisms to report:

- Phishing
- Suspicious messages
- Lost devices
- Security incidents
- Data exposure
- Policy concerns
- Physical security issues

Reporting mechanisms should be easy to find and use.

---

## 45. Psychological Safety in Security

A strong reporting culture should allow people to report mistakes and suspicious events without unnecessary fear of punishment.

This can improve:

- Early reporting
- Incident visibility
- Learning
- Corrective action
- Organizational resilience

Accountability remains important, but security programs should distinguish intentional misconduct from honest mistakes.

---

## 46. Security Culture and Leadership

Leadership influences security culture through:

- Visible support
- Resource allocation
- Consistent expectations
- Example-setting
- Communication
- Accountability

Security culture is difficult to sustain when leadership behavior contradicts organizational security expectations.

## 52. Joiner, Mover and Leaver Processes

### Joiner

Provide appropriate access.

### Mover

### Leaver

These processes connect human resources, identity management, security, and business operations.

## 74. Key Takeaways

Cybersecurity is a socio-technical discipline.

Security outcomes are influenced by:

- People
- Technology
- Processes
- Organizational culture
- Incentives
- Communication
- Architecture
- Governance

Effective security therefore requires more than technical controls.

It requires designing systems and processes that help people make secure decisions and recover effectively when mistakes or attacks occur.

---

## 75. Professional Perspective

The human element should not be treated simply as the "weakest link."

People can also be:

- Sensors
- Reporters
- Decision-makers
- Security defenders
- Sources of organizational resilience
- Contributors to security improvement

A mature cybersecurity program designs security around realistic human behavior while maintaining appropriate accountability and controls.

The objective is not to eliminate human involvement.

The objective is to create systems, processes, and cultures in which secure behavior is practical, supported, and reinforced.
