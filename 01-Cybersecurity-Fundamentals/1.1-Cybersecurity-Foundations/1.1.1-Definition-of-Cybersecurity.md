1.1.1 Definition of Cybersecurity

1. Overview

Cybersecurity is a multidisciplinary discipline concerned with protecting digital systems, information, services, and interconnected environments against cyber threats. It combines people, processes, technology, governance, and risk management to reduce the likelihood and impact of cyber incidents.

Cybersecurity applies throughout the lifecycle of a system: planning, design, development, deployment, operation, maintenance, and retirement.

2. Definition

Cybersecurity is the practice of managing cyber risks and protecting systems, networks, applications, devices, services, and information against unauthorized access, misuse, disruption, modification, destruction, and other malicious or unintended events. It includes prevention, detection, response, recovery, and continuous improvement.

This is a synthesized learning definition informed by established cybersecurity terminology and frameworks. It is not a verbatim quotation from a single standard.

Cybersecurity is not limited to preventing attacks. It also includes preparing for incidents, limiting their consequences, restoring essential services, and learning from failures.

3. Scope of Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity protects both digital information and the systems that create, process, store, transmit, and act upon it.

3.1 Information and data

- Personal and sensitive information
- Business records and intellectual property
- Credentials, cryptographic keys, and configuration data
- Operational data and control-system information

3.2 Technology and infrastructure

- End-user devices, servers, and operating systems
- Networks, routers, switches, and communication links
- Applications, APIs, and software supply chains
- Cloud platforms, containers, and virtual infrastructure
- Internet of Things (IoT) devices
- Operational technology (OT), industrial control systems (ICS), and cyber-physical systems (CPS)

3.3 People and organizational processes

- Identity and access management
- Security awareness and responsibilities
- Policies, standards, and procedures
- Third-party and supply-chain relationships
- Incident response and business continuity

4. Core Security Objectives

Cybersecurity commonly uses the Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA) triad as a foundational model.

Objective| Meaning| Example
Confidentiality| Information is accessible only to authorized entities.| Encrypting sensitive records and enforcing access controls.
Integrity| Information and system states are protected against unauthorized or improper changes.| Verifying software updates and validating transaction records.
Availability| Systems and information are accessible when required by authorized users.| Redundant services, tested backups, and denial-of-service protections.

Other important security properties include authenticity, accountability, traceability, and non-repudiation. Their relevance depends on the system, threat model, and business requirements.

The CIA triad is foundational, but it does not by itself describe every security, safety, privacy, or resilience requirement.

5. Cybersecurity Across Technology Environments

5.1 Information technology (IT)

Focuses on business computing, endpoints, enterprise networks, applications, identity systems, and data.

5.2 Cloud security

Addresses cloud identities, configurations, workloads, data protection, service interfaces, and shared-responsibility boundaries.

5.3 Internet of Things (IoT) security

Protects connected devices, firmware, device identities, communication channels, update mechanisms, and associated cloud services.

5.4 Operational technology (OT) and industrial control systems (ICS)

Protects systems that monitor or control physical processes, such as programmable logic controllers (PLCs), supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems, and industrial networks.

Security decisions in OT must consider operational availability, safety, reliability, maintenance constraints, and the consequences of changing or interrupting a process.

5.5 Cyber-physical systems (CPS)

CPS integrate computational components with physical processes. Examples include connected medical equipment, industrial automation, smart buildings, and transportation systems.

A cyber incident affecting a CPS may have consequences beyond data loss, including equipment damage, service interruption, environmental harm, or risks to human safety.

6. Cybersecurity as Risk Management

Cybersecurity is not simply a collection of tools. It is a continuous risk-management activity.

A typical process includes:

1. Identify assets, business objectives, dependencies, and critical services.
2. Understand threats, vulnerabilities, exposure, and potential consequences.
3. Evaluate risk using likelihood, impact, and organizational context.
4. Select and implement appropriate safeguards.
5. Monitor systems and detect suspicious or harmful activity.
6. Respond to incidents and recover affected services.
7. Evaluate effectiveness and improve the security posture.

Controls should be proportionate to the risk and aligned with legal obligations, contractual commitments, operational requirements, and business priorities.

7. Cybersecurity Lifecycle

Security should be considered throughout the system lifecycle rather than added only after deployment.

- Govern and plan: establish accountability, objectives, policies, and risk tolerance.
- Design: define security requirements, trust boundaries, and threat models.
- Build and acquire: use secure development, configuration, testing, and supply-chain practices.
- Deploy: harden systems, configure identities, and validate controls.
- Operate and monitor: maintain visibility, patch systems, review access, and detect threats.
- Respond and recover: contain incidents, restore trusted operations, and learn from events.
- Retire: remove access, securely dispose of data, and decommission assets.

These activities are continuous and may overlap. They are a learning model, not a replacement for a specific lifecycle standard.

8. Practical Example

Scenario: A connected water-treatment facility

A facility uses industrial controllers, sensors, operator workstations, and a supervisory system to monitor water-treatment processes.

Potential cybersecurity risks include:

- Unauthorized access to operator accounts
- Malicious or unsafe changes to controller logic
- Ransomware affecting supervisory workstations
- Loss or manipulation of sensor data
- Network disruption that reduces operational visibility

A risk-based security approach could include:

- Asset inventory and documented system dependencies
- Segmentation between enterprise IT and industrial control networks
- Strong authentication and controlled privileged access
- Authorized change management for controller configurations
- Monitoring of critical communications and system events
- Tested backups and recovery procedures
- Coordination between cybersecurity, engineering, operations, and safety personnel

The objective is not merely to protect computers. It is to reduce cyber risk while maintaining safe and reliable water-treatment operations.

9. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: Cybersecurity means installing antivirus software.

Correction: Endpoint protection is one control among many. Effective cybersecurity also requires architecture, identity controls, secure configuration, monitoring, incident response, recovery, and governance.

Misconception 2: A system is secure if no attack has been detected.

Correction: Absence of detected incidents is not proof of security. Monitoring gaps, unknown vulnerabilities, and undetected compromise may remain.

Misconception 3: Cybersecurity is only an IT responsibility.

Correction: Cybersecurity requires shared responsibility across leadership, engineering, IT, operations, procurement, legal, risk, and other relevant functions.

Misconception 4: Compliance automatically means security.

Correction: Compliance demonstrates alignment with defined requirements. It does not guarantee that all relevant threats and risks are adequately managed.

Misconception 5: Security and availability are always opposing goals.

Correction: Some controls introduce operational overhead, but well-designed architecture can improve both security and availability. Trade-offs must be evaluated in context.

10. Architecture and Engineering Perspective

A cybersecurity architect translates business and operational risks into security requirements and system designs.

Key activities include:

- Defining system boundaries, assets, interfaces, and dependencies
- Identifying trust boundaries and data flows
- Performing threat modelling and attack-surface analysis
- Selecting preventive, detective, responsive, and recovery controls
- Applying least privilege and appropriate segmentation
- Defining secure configuration, logging, monitoring, and recovery requirements
- Validating that controls meet security and operational objectives
- Documenting assumptions, residual risks, and design decisions

Security architecture should be risk-informed, measurable, maintainable, and appropriate to the environment.

11. Governance and Business Perspective

Cybersecurity decisions affect business continuity, customer trust, regulatory exposure, financial loss, safety, and organizational reputation.

Governance establishes:

- Security accountability and decision authority
- Policies and risk-management expectations
- Resource allocation and investment priorities
- Risk acceptance and escalation procedures
- Performance measurement and assurance
- Oversight of suppliers and external dependencies

Security teams should communicate risk in terms decision-makers can evaluate, including business impact, likelihood, control effectiveness, cost, and residual risk.

12. Relationship to Other Disciplines

Cybersecurity overlaps with several related disciplines, but their scopes and objectives are not identical.

- Information security: protects information and information systems, including digital and potentially non-digital information.
- IT security: focuses on information technology assets and environments.
- Privacy: concerns appropriate handling of personal information and the risks arising from its processing.
- Safety engineering: addresses unacceptable risks of harm to people, property, and the environment.
- Resilience: concerns the ability to withstand disruption, adapt, recover, and continue delivering important outcomes.

The boundaries vary by organization and context. The distinctions are explored in the following micro-topics.

13. Key Takeaways

1. Cybersecurity is a continuous discipline for managing cyber risk, not just a collection of security products.
2. Its scope includes information, systems, people, processes, technology, and interconnected dependencies.
3. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability provide a foundational model for security objectives.
4. Effective cybersecurity combines prevention, detection, response, recovery, governance, and improvement.
5. IT, cloud, IoT, OT, and cyber-physical environments have different operational and risk considerations.
6. Security architecture translates risk and requirements into implementable, testable safeguards.
7. Compliance, tools, and the absence of known incidents do not independently prove that a system is secure.
