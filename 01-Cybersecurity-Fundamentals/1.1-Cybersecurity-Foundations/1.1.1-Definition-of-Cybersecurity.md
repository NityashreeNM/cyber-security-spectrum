# 1.1.1 — Definition of Cybersecurity

## 1. Overview

Cybersecurity is the discipline of protecting digital systems, information, services, and interconnected environments against cyber risks.

It combines:

- People
- Processes
- Technology
- Governance
- Risk management
- Incident response
- Recovery and improvement

Cybersecurity applies throughout the system lifecycle, including planning, design, development, deployment, operation, maintenance, and retirement.

It is not limited to preventing attacks. It also includes detecting harmful activity, responding to incidents, limiting consequences, restoring trusted operations, and improving defenses after failure.

## 2. Definition

Cybersecurity is the continuous practice of managing cyber risks and protecting systems, networks, applications, devices, services, and information against:

- Unauthorized access
- Unauthorized use
- Misuse
- Interruption
- Modification
- Destruction
- Disclosure
- Loss

The purpose of cybersecurity is to reduce risk to an acceptable level while supporting organizational, operational, privacy, safety, and business objectives.

This is a synthesized learning definition based on established cybersecurity terminology. It is not a quotation from a specific standard.

## 3. Scope of Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity protects both digital information and the systems that create, process, store, transmit, and use it.

### 3.1 Information and Data

Cybersecurity covers:

- Personal and sensitive information
- Business records
- Intellectual property
- Credentials and cryptographic keys
- Configuration data
- Operational and control-system information
- Records required for legal, regulatory, or business purposes

### 3.2 Systems and Technology

Cybersecurity covers:

- End-user devices
- Servers and operating systems
- Networks and communication links
- Applications and application programming interfaces
- Cloud platforms and virtual infrastructure
- Software supply chains
- Internet of Things devices
- Operational technology and industrial control systems
- Cyber-physical systems

### 3.3 People and Processes

Technology alone cannot provide security. Cybersecurity also depends on:

- Security awareness
- Clearly assigned responsibilities
- Policies and procedures
- Identity and access management
- Change management
- Secure operations
- Supplier management
- Incident response
- Business continuity
- Recovery planning
- Continuous improvement

## 4. Core Security Objectives

Cybersecurity commonly uses the **Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA) triad** as a foundational model.

| Objective | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Protect information from unauthorized access or disclosure | Restrict access to medical records |
| Integrity | Prevent unauthorized or improper modification | Validate financial transactions |
| Availability | Ensure authorized access when required | Maintain access to emergency services |
| Authenticity | Confirm that identities, communications, and resources are genuine | Verify a user or software publisher |
| Accountability | Attribute actions to responsible entities | Use individual accounts and protected audit records |
| Traceability | Follow activities and changes through records | Investigate an unauthorized configuration change |
| Non-repudiation | Preserve evidence supporting the origin or occurrence of an action | Use a digital signature for a transaction |

The CIA triad is foundational but does not represent every privacy, safety, resilience, or compliance requirement.

## 5. Cybersecurity as Risk Management

Cybersecurity is primarily a continuous risk-management discipline rather than a collection of security products.

A practical risk-management process includes:

1. Identify important assets and business requirements.
2. Identify dependencies and critical services.
3. Identify threats, vulnerabilities, exposures, and attack surfaces.
4. Assess potential likelihood and consequences.
5. Select controls proportionate to the risk.
6. Implement and verify the controls.
7. Monitor systems and detect harmful activity.
8. Respond to incidents and recover affected services.
9. Evaluate performance and improve the security posture.

Controls should align with:

- Risk tolerance
- Business objectives
- Operational requirements
- Legal and contractual obligations
- Safety requirements
- Available resources
- Residual risk

Installing a security tool does not automatically achieve a security objective. Controls must be configured, operated, monitored, tested, and evaluated.

## 6. Cybersecurity Across Technology Environments

### 6.1 Information Technology

Enterprise IT includes endpoints, servers, networks, applications, identity systems, databases, and business services.

Security priorities include protecting business information, controlling access, maintaining reliable applications, preventing unauthorized changes, and recovering from incidents.

### 6.2 Cloud Security

Cloud security addresses:

- Identity and access management
- Cloud configurations
- Workloads and containers
- Stored and transmitted data
- Service interfaces
- Network exposure
- Shared-responsibility boundaries
- Logging and monitoring
- Supply-chain dependencies

Cloud use does not transfer all security responsibility to the cloud provider. Responsibilities depend on the service model and contractual arrangement.

### 6.3 Internet of Things Security

IoT security protects:

- Devices and firmware
- Device identities
- Communication channels
- Update mechanisms
- Storage and credentials
- Associated cloud and mobile services
- Data collected from physical environments

IoT systems may have limited processing capacity, long lifetimes, weak update mechanisms, or difficult physical access. Security must account for these limitations.

### 6.4 Operational Technology Security

Operational technology includes systems that monitor or control physical processes, including:

- Programmable logic controllers
- Supervisory control and data acquisition systems
- Industrial networks
- Distributed control systems
- Building and infrastructure automation

OT security decisions must consider:

- Operational availability
- Safety
- Reliability
- Process continuity
- Maintenance requirements
- Testing limitations
- Consequences of interruption or unauthorized changes

### 6.5 Cyber-Physical System Security

Cyber-physical systems combine computing, communication, control, and physical processes.

Examples include:

- Connected medical devices
- Industrial automation
- Intelligent transportation
- Smart buildings
- Environmental control systems

A cyber incident may cause:

- Equipment damage
- Process disruption
- Environmental harm
- Financial loss
- Service outages
- Injury or loss of life

## 7. Cybersecurity Lifecycle

Security should be considered from planning through retirement.

| Lifecycle phase | Security activities |
|---|---|
| Govern and plan | Define objectives, accountability, policies, risk tolerance, and resources |
| Design | Define requirements, trust boundaries, dependencies, and threat scenarios |
| Acquire and build | Evaluate suppliers, use secure development, and test security capabilities |
| Deploy | Harden systems, configure identities, validate controls, and document assumptions |
| Operate | Patch systems, manage access, monitor activity, and maintain configurations |
| Respond | Detect, contain, investigate, communicate, and coordinate incident handling |
| Recover | Restore services, validate integrity, and learn from incidents |
| Retire | Remove access, securely dispose of data, and decommission assets |

These phases are continuous and often overlap. They provide a learning model rather than replacing an organization’s specific lifecycle framework.

## 8. Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1: Cybersecurity means installing antivirus software

Antivirus protection is only one preventive control. Effective cybersecurity also requires identity protection, secure configuration, monitoring, risk management, incident response, recovery, and governance.

### Misconception 2: A system is secure if no attack is detected

Absence of detected incidents does not prove security. Monitoring gaps, unknown vulnerabilities, misconfiguration, or undetected compromise may exist.

### Misconception 3: Cybersecurity is only an IT responsibility

Cybersecurity requires shared responsibility across leadership, engineering, operations, IT, procurement, legal, human resources, risk management, and users.

### Misconception 4: Compliance automatically means security

Compliance demonstrates alignment with defined requirements. It does not guarantee that every relevant threat or risk is adequately managed.

### Misconception 5: Security and availability are always opposed

Poorly designed controls can create availability problems. However, well-designed security architecture can improve reliability, incident detection, and recovery while maintaining necessary availability.

## 9. Relationship to Other Disciplines

Cybersecurity overlaps with several disciplines, but their scopes are not identical.

- **Information security:** Protects information and information systems in digital or non-digital forms.
- **IT security:** Protects information technology assets and technology environments.
- **Privacy:** Governs appropriate collection, use, sharing, retention, and disposal of personal information.
- **Safety engineering:** Prevents unacceptable harm to people, property, or the environment.
- **Resilience:** Enables an organization or system to withstand disruption, adapt, recover, and continue essential operations.
- **Risk management:** Evaluates uncertainty and supports decisions about threats, impacts, controls, and risk acceptance.

The relationship between these disciplines varies by organization and context.

## 10. Architecture and Governance Perspective

A cybersecurity architect translates business and operational risks into security requirements and system designs.

Key activities include:

- Defining assets, boundaries, interfaces, and dependencies
- Identifying trust boundaries and data flows
- Performing threat modelling
- Selecting preventive, detective, corrective, and recovery controls
- Applying least privilege
- Defining logging and monitoring requirements
- Planning recovery
- Documenting assumptions, trade-offs, and residual risks
- Verifying whether controls meet operational and security objectives

Governance establishes accountability and decision-making through:

- Policies and standards
- Defined roles and responsibilities
- Security objectives
- Risk tolerance
- Resource allocation
- Risk acceptance
- Performance measurement
- Supplier oversight
- Independent assurance and review

## 11. Key Takeaways

1. Cybersecurity is a continuous discipline for managing cyber risk.
2. Its scope includes information, systems, people, processes, and dependencies.
3. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability provide a foundational security model.
4. Authenticity, accountability, privacy, safety, traceability, and resilience may also be required.
5. Cybersecurity covers prevention, detection, response, recovery, governance, and improvement.
6. IT, cloud, IoT, OT, and cyber-physical environments have different operational risks.
7. Security architecture translates objectives and risks into implementable controls.
8. Compliance, tools, and the absence of detected incidents do not independently prove that a system is secure.
