# 1.1.6 — Cybersecurity vs. Privacy

## 1. Overview

Cybersecurity and privacy are closely related, but they address different types of risk.

**Cybersecurity** manages risks to systems, networks, applications, devices, information, and services by protecting them against unauthorized access, misuse, disruption, modification, and loss.

**Privacy** governs how personal information is collected, used, shared, retained, and disposed of, including whether that processing is lawful, transparent, necessary, fair, and appropriate.

A system can be highly secure yet violate privacy, and a privacy program can be difficult to operate without adequate cybersecurity.

## 2. Definitions

### 2.1 Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity applies technical, organizational, and operational safeguards to manage cyber risks.

Common cybersecurity objectives include:

- Confidentiality
- Integrity
- Availability
- Authenticity
- Accountability
- Traceability
- Resilience
- Secure recovery

Cybersecurity asks questions such as:

- Who or what can access the system?
- Can the system resist attack?
- Can unauthorized activity be detected?
- Can an incident be contained?
- Can data and services be restored?
- Are systems configured and monitored appropriately?

### 2.2 Privacy

Privacy concerns individual rights and organizational responsibilities involving personal information.

Privacy asks questions such as:

- Why is personal information collected?
- Is collection and use lawful?
- Is the processing necessary for a stated purpose?
- Are individuals informed appropriately?
- Is the information accurate and limited to what is needed?
- Who is allowed to receive or process it?
- How long is the information retained?
- Can an individual access, correct, or delete information where applicable?

Privacy requirements depend on applicable law and organizational policies.

## 3. Key Differences

| Area | Cybersecurity | Privacy |
|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | Protecting systems and information from cyber threats | Appropriate processing of personal information |
| Main concern | Unauthorized access, misuse, disruption, or loss | Lawfulness, fairness, purpose, transparency, and individual rights |
| Primary subject | Systems, data, identities, networks, and services | Personal information and the people to whom it relates |
| Core question | Is information and the system protected? | Is personal information handled appropriately? |
| Typical controls | Encryption, access control, monitoring, patching, backup | Consent or other lawful basis, data minimization, notices, retention controls |
| Risk examples | Breach, ransomware, intrusion, account compromise | Excessive collection, improper use, unlawful sharing, poor retention |
| Responsible functions | Security, IT, engineering, operations, risk | Privacy, legal, compliance, data owners, business operations |

Cybersecurity and privacy overlap, but neither automatically guarantees the other.

## 4. Personal Information in Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity protects personal information as one type of valuable or sensitive information.

Examples of personal information include:

- Names and addresses
- Identification numbers
- Phone numbers and email addresses
- Medical information
- Financial records
- Account information
- Location data
- Biometric information
- Photos or audio recordings
- Online identifiers
- Employment records

Personal information may require both privacy and security controls.

**Example:** A healthcare application should:

- Use access controls to prevent unauthorized access
- Encrypt sensitive records
- Log and monitor access
- Apply retention limits
- Display an appropriate privacy notice
- Process information only for authorized purposes
- Support applicable individual rights
- Securely delete or anonymize records when permitted and required

Encryption and access control address cybersecurity objectives. Purpose limitation and data retention address privacy objectives.

## 5. Areas of Overlap

Cybersecurity and privacy share several controls and responsibilities.

### Shared responsibilities

Both disciplines require:

- Identifying sensitive information
- Assigning clear ownership
- Defining use and access rules
- Protecting data throughout its lifecycle
- Evaluating suppliers and processors
- Monitoring for misuse
- Responding to incidents
- Limiting unnecessary access
- Maintaining accurate records
- Supporting secure deletion
- Training employees
- Reporting and managing risk

### Shared controls

Examples include:

- Least-privilege access
- Encryption
- Secure configuration
- Data classification
- Authentication
- Logging and auditability
- Data-loss prevention
- Incident response
- Secure backups
- Vendor reviews
- Access and retention reviews
- Privacy and security impact assessments

## 6. Privacy Without Cybersecurity

A privacy program may include strong policies but fail to prevent unauthorized access.

**Example:** An organization publishes a clear privacy policy and defines acceptable uses for customer information. However, it does not:

- Protect accounts with strong authentication
- Restrict employees to necessary information
- Encrypt sensitive data
- Monitor unauthorized exports
- Manage third-party access
- Securely dispose of obsolete records
- Respond to unauthorized disclosure

The organization may fail both privacy obligations and cybersecurity requirements.

## 7. Cybersecurity Without Privacy

A technically secure system may still process personal information inappropriately.

**Example:** An application:

- Uses strong encryption
- Requires multi-factor authentication
- Maintains extensive logs
- Restricts administrators
- Receives no evidence of unauthorized access

Despite these controls, it may collect location data for purposes not described to users or retain records longer than necessary.

The system is protected against many cyber threats, but its use of personal information may still create a privacy risk.

## 8. Data Protection Principles

Privacy frameworks commonly address principles such as:

- Lawfulness
- Fairness
- Transparency
- Purpose limitation
- Data minimization
- Accuracy
- Storage limitation
- Integrity and confidentiality
- Accountability
- Individual rights
- Restricted processing

Cybersecurity supports the integrity, confidentiality, and availability aspects of privacy. Privacy principles determine whether processing is appropriate beyond the protection of the system.

The exact principles and obligations depend on jurisdiction, contract, organizational policy, and the type of information involved.

## 9. Cybersecurity Incident and Privacy Breach

A cybersecurity incident does not always become a privacy breach.

For example, a ransomware incident could affect publicly available system files containing no personal information. The incident is primarily a cybersecurity event, although reporting and continuity duties may still apply.

A privacy breach involves actual or potential inappropriate handling of personal information.

Examples include:

- Personal records exposed through an unprotected database
- A misdirected email containing employee data
- An account used to view records without authorization
- Excessive internal access to customer records
- Personal data shared with an unauthorized vendor
- Stolen or lost devices containing identifiable information
- Improper deletion or retention of personal information

A single event may be both a cybersecurity incident and a privacy breach.

## 10. Responsibilities and Roles

### Cybersecurity responsibilities

Cybersecurity teams commonly:

- Identify security threats and vulnerabilities
- Protect accounts and systems
- Design technical safeguards
- Monitor activity
- Detect and investigate cyber incidents
- Contain compromise
- Restore systems and data
- Maintain recovery capabilities
- Report cyber risk
- Work with incident-response and legal teams

### Privacy responsibilities

Privacy teams commonly:

- Define privacy obligations and organizational rules
- Review data-processing activities
- Manage privacy notices and preferences
- Evaluate lawful processing
- Support data subject requests
- Review retention and deletion requirements
- Coordinate privacy impact assessments
- Determine privacy reporting and notification needs
- Coordinate with legal and regulatory teams
- Oversee responsible data use

Privacy and cybersecurity teams should coordinate during system design, incident response, supplier review, and breach investigation.

## 11. Privacy by Design and Security by Design

### Privacy by design

Privacy by design means considering privacy obligations and risks during planning and implementation.

It includes:

- Minimizing collected data
- Limiting use and disclosure
- Defining appropriate retention
- Supporting individual rights
- Making default settings privacy-conscious
- Conducting privacy reviews
- Managing third-party access
- Reviewing processing when requirements change

### Security by design

Security by design means considering cybersecurity risks throughout planning and implementation.

It includes:

- Defining assets and trust boundaries
- Threat modelling
- Applying least privilege
- Protecting data
- Logging and monitoring
- Secure development
- Recovery planning
- Testing controls

The two approaches should be combined so that systems are both protected and appropriately used.

## 12. Privacy Threat Modelling

A privacy review may identify issues that a traditional cybersecurity threat model does not.

Examples include:

- Collection of more data than needed
- Use of data for a different purpose
- Storage of identifiable information for too long
- Inadequate transparency
- Profiling or automated decisions
- Excessive tracking
- Inappropriate reidentification
- Transfer of data across unapproved boundaries
- Vendor access outside the authorized purpose
- Unclear ownership of sensitive data

A privacy threat model should consider both technical compromise and inappropriate or unapproved processing.

## 13. Incidents and Breach Response

During a security incident, an organization should determine both the cybersecurity impact and the privacy impact.

Relevant questions include:

- Was personal information involved?
- Did unauthorized parties access or modify it?
- Was the information encrypted or protected?
- Which individuals or systems are affected?
- Is the incident ongoing?
- What immediate containment actions are needed?
- What legal or contractual reporting duties apply?
- Which records must be preserved?
- What notification or communication is required?
- How should affected individuals, regulators, suppliers, or partners be informed?

Privacy and cybersecurity response plans should be coordinated rather than handled separately.

## 14. Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1: Encryption guarantees privacy

**Correction:** Encryption protects information against unauthorized access, but does not determine whether collection, use, disclosure, or retention is appropriate.

### Misconception 2: Privacy is only a legal requirement

**Correction:** Legal compliance is important, but privacy also involves trust, ethics, data governance, individual rights, and organizational responsibility.

### Misconception 3: Cybersecurity teams handle all privacy work

**Correction:** Cybersecurity provides important safeguards, but privacy obligations require legal, policy, business, and data-governance activities.

### Misconception 4: An authorized employee is a privacy-compliant use

**Correction:** Employee authorization may provide technical access but may not permit all legitimate or lawful uses of personal information.

### Misconception 5: Privacy and security are interchangeable

**Correction:** Privacy governs appropriate information processing, while cybersecurity manages risks to systems and information.

## 15. Key Takeaways

1. Cybersecurity protects systems and information from cyber risks.
2. Privacy governs appropriate collection, use, sharing, retention, and disposal of personal information.
3. Cybersecurity and privacy overlap and support each other.
4. Strong security does not guarantee privacy.
5. A privacy-compliant process may still require stronger cybersecurity controls.
6. Privacy and security should be considered together during system design.
7. Incident response should evaluate cybersecurity, privacy, legal, and notification implications.
