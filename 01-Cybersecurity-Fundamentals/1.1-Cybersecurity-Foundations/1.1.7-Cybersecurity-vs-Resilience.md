# 1.1.7 — Cybersecurity vs. Resilience

## 1. Overview

Cybersecurity and resilience are related but distinct concepts.

**Cybersecurity** manages cyber risks and protects systems, information, identities, services, and connected environments from unauthorized access, misuse, disruption, modification, and loss.

**Resilience** is the ability to anticipate, withstand, adapt to, recover from, and learn from adverse conditions while continuing to deliver important outcomes.

Cybersecurity helps prevent, detect, contain, and recover from cyber events. Resilience helps an organization continue essential operations when disruption occurs, including when security controls fail.

## 2. Key Differences

| Area | Cybersecurity | Resilience |
|---|---|---|
| Primary focus | Managing cyber risks and threats | Continuing essential outcomes during disruption |
| Main objective | Protect systems, information, and operations | Withstand disruption and recover |
| Main concern | Cyber attacks and security failures | Cyber and non-cyber disruptions |
| Typical measures | Access control, patching, monitoring, incident response | Continuity planning, redundancy, alternatives, recovery |
| Success indicator | Risk and unauthorized activity are controlled | Critical functions continue or recover acceptably |
| Scope | Cyber-dependent systems and organizations | The organization and its people, processes, technology, dependencies |
| Time perspective | Before, during, and after cyber events | Preparation, disruption, recovery, adaptation |

Cybersecurity is often a key component of resilience, but resilience is broader.

## 3. Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity includes:

- Risk assessment
- Governance and policies
- Security architecture
- Identity and access management
- Vulnerability and configuration management
- Network and application security
- Monitoring and threat detection
- Incident response
- Evidence preservation
- Recovery and restoration
- Lessons learned and improvement

Cybersecurity seeks to reduce:

- The likelihood of compromise
- The opportunity for unauthorized access
- The duration of harmful activity
- The impact of incidents
- The risk of unsafe or unreliable operation

Cybersecurity cannot guarantee that every event will be prevented. Its objective is to manage risk to an acceptable level.

## 4. Resilience

Resilience includes the ability to:

- Identify critical outcomes and dependencies
- Prepare for disruption
- Operate with reduced or degraded capability
- Continue essential functions
- Coordinate people and external parties
- Restore trusted operations
- Adapt processes and controls
- Learn from incidents and exercises
- Improve future readiness

Resilience applies to cyber incidents, but also to:

- Natural disasters
- Utility failures
- Supply-chain interruptions
- Equipment failures
- Human error
- Pandemics or public emergencies
- Loss of facilities or communications
- Staff shortages
- Operational and financial disruptions

## 5. How Cybersecurity Supports Resilience

### 5.1 Preventing disruption

Preventive cybersecurity controls reduce exposure to attacks:

- Least privilege
- Secure authentication
- Network segmentation
- Secure configuration
- Patch management
- Application security
- Email and endpoint protection
- Data protection
- Supplier risk management

### 5.2 Detecting disruption

Monitoring helps identify incidents before they cause greater harm:

- Security logs
- Endpoint and network detection
- Identity monitoring
- Application monitoring
- Alerting and incident escalation
- Anomaly detection
- Vulnerability monitoring

### 5.3 Limiting impact

Incident-response capabilities reduce the consequences of compromise:

- Incident classification
- Account isolation
- System containment
- Communication and coordination
- Evidence preservation
- Threat eradication
- Controlled restoration
- Post-incident improvement

### 5.4 Supporting recovery

Recovery depends on trustworthy and tested capabilities:

- Verified backups
- Restoration procedures
- Recovery objectives
- Configuration documentation
- Secure reconstruction
- Independent recovery environments
- Alternative operating procedures
- Validation of restored information

## 6. How Resilience Strengthens Cybersecurity

Resilience recognizes that some controls will fail or be bypassed.

An organization may use strong authentication and endpoint security, but also needs:

- Monitoring for unusual activity
- Segmentation to limit lateral movement
- Tested incident procedures
- Secure and available backups
- Alternative communications
- Manual or degraded operating methods
- Prioritized recovery
- Exercises to test readiness
- Processes for learning and improvement

Resilience therefore complements prevention and assumes that successful attacks or unavoidable disruption may occur.

## 7. Availability and Resilience

Availability and resilience are closely related but not identical.

### Availability

Availability means that authorized users can access a system or information when required.

### Resilience

Resilience means the organization can continue important outcomes or restore them after disruption, even if normal systems are unavailable.

A system may have high availability but weak organizational resilience. For example:

- The backup and primary systems may share the same vulnerability.
- Recovery staff may not be available.
- Backups may be incomplete or corrupted.
- Critical suppliers may not support recovery.
- Restoration priorities may be unclear.
- Communications may fail.
- Operators may not have an alternative procedure.
- Legal or safety requirements may restrict recovery.

## 8. Cybersecurity and Risk

Both disciplines support risk management, but they emphasize different stages.

### Cybersecurity risk management

Cybersecurity seeks to:

- Identify assets and threats
- Assess vulnerabilities and exposure
- Implement preventive controls
- Detect harmful activity
- Respond to incidents
- Restore trusted operations
- Improve controls

### Resilience risk management

Resilience seeks to:

- Identify critical outcomes
- Identify dependencies
- Define acceptable disruption
- Establish continuity priorities
- Prepare alternatives
- Support recovery
- Adapt to changing conditions
- Learn from events and exercises

A complete risk approach may ask:

- What assets and services are critical?
- What threats could affect them?
- Which controls reduce likelihood or impact?
- What happens if the controls fail?
- Which functions must continue?
- How quickly must they recover?
- Which dependencies may prevent recovery?
- What acceptable degraded operation is possible?
- How will the organization improve?

## 9. Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Two important resilience disciplines are business continuity and disaster recovery.

### 9.1 Business Continuity

Business continuity helps an organization continue essential functions during disruption.

Examples include:

- Alternate work locations
- Manual processing procedures
- Emergency communication
- Alternate suppliers
- Staffing and delegation plans
- Prioritized service delivery
- Coordination with public authorities or partners

### 9.2 Disaster Recovery

Disaster recovery focuses on restoring information technology systems and services after a disaster or major incident.

Examples include:

- Rebuilding servers and applications
- Restoring network configurations
- Recovering data
- Reestablishing identity systems
- Validating restored integrity
- Performing recovery tests
- Reconstructing systems securely

Business continuity may focus on organizational operations. Disaster recovery may focus on technology restoration. Both depend on security controls and trustworthy recovery processes.

## 10. Resilience in Different Environments

### Enterprise IT

Important resilience considerations include:

- Critical application dependencies
- Identity-system recovery
- Data-center and cloud redundancy
- Backup and restoration
- Alternative communications
- Prioritized restoration
- Supplier support
- Tested incident procedures

### Cloud environments

Cloud resilience depends on:

- Region and availability-zone choices
- Provider and service dependencies
- Identity-system dependencies
- Data replication and backup
- Configuration recovery
- Shared-responsibility boundaries
- Supplier outages
- Controlled restoration

Cloud availability features do not remove the organization’s recovery and continuity responsibilities.

### Operational technology

OT resilience is strongly associated with:

- Maintaining safe physical processes
- Limiting industrial disruption
- Preserving monitoring and control
- Protecting emergency functions
- Supporting manual operation where appropriate
- Maintaining accurate process state
- Coordinating engineering and operations
- Testing recovery without unsafe interruption

Cybersecurity controls must be compatible with safety and operational requirements.

### Cyber-physical systems

Cyber-physical systems may require resilience planning for:

- Equipment damage
- Loss of measurement or control
- Environmental harm
- Personal safety
- Physical service interruption
- Reliable restoration
- Safe degraded operation
- Preservation of essential functions

## 11. Recovery Time and Recovery Point Objectives

Organizations should define measurable recovery requirements.

### Recovery Time Objective

**Recovery Time Objective (RTO)** is the target time to restore a system or service after disruption.

**Example:** A critical application must be restored within four hours.

### Recovery Point Objective

**Recovery Point Objective (RPO)** is the maximum acceptable period of data loss.

**Example:** Financial records must be backed up frequently enough to limit loss to 30 minutes.

RTO and RPO should reflect:

- Mission and customer requirements
- Operational consequences
- Safety requirements
- Regulatory obligations
- Financial impact
- Technical feasibility
- Recovery dependencies
- The cost of controls

A short RTO or RPO may require investment in redundancy, replication, or improved recovery processes.

## 12. Security and Resilience Trade-offs

Security and resilience activities may require trade-offs, but they should not be treated as automatically opposing goals.

### Potential tensions

Examples include:

- Additional security checks delaying access
- Isolation reducing immediate availability
- Recovery from a trusted backup taking longer than emergency access
- Stronger logging consuming system capacity
- Segmentation limiting convenient communication
- Redundant systems creating additional attack surfaces

### Risk-based decisions

Organizations should evaluate:

- The risk being reduced
- The operational impact
- The safety impact
- The recovery impact
- The cost and maintainability
- The likelihood and consequence of failure
- Alternative or compensating controls
- Residual risk

Well-designed security architecture can improve both security and resilience.

## 13. Measuring Cybersecurity and Resilience

Cybersecurity and resilience require different indicators.

### Cybersecurity indicators

Examples include:

- Percentage of critical vulnerabilities addressed
- Patch completion time
- Unapproved configuration changes
- Time to detect and contain incidents
- Coverage of security monitoring
- Percentage of accounts using required authentication
- Effectiveness of security controls

### Resilience indicators

Examples include:

- Time to restore critical services
- Achievement of recovery objectives
- Success of continuity exercises
- Percentage of critical dependencies mapped
- Time to activate alternate operations
- Recovery test success
- Lessons completed after exercises or incidents
- Availability of trained recovery personnel

Metrics should be interpreted with context. A high system availability percentage does not by itself demonstrate organizational resilience.

## 14. Practical Example

Consider a hospital information system.

**Cybersecurity controls** may include:

- Identity and access control
- Multi-factor authentication
- Endpoint protection
- Network segmentation
- Security monitoring
- Vulnerability management
- Protected backups
- Incident response
- Secure remote access

**Resilience capabilities** may include:

- A documented alternative for critical clinical functions
- Backup power
- Local access to urgent information
- Manual emergency procedures
- Prioritized restoration
- Coordination with clinical leadership
- Tested recovery processes
- Procedures for temporary loss of network access
- Supplier and communications support

A hospital can have strong cybersecurity controls but still lack resilience if staff cannot provide safe care during an outage.

## 15. Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1: Cybersecurity automatically provides resilience

**Correction:** Cybersecurity is necessary for reducing cyber risk, but resilience also requires continuity planning, alternatives, recovery, and organizational coordination.

### Misconception 2: Resilience means never experiencing disruption

**Correction:** Resilience means reducing harm and continuing important outcomes when disruption occurs.

### Misconception 3: Resilience means ignoring prevention

**Correction:** Prevention reduces the frequency and impact of events. Resilience assumes prevention may fail and prepares for that possibility.

### Misconception 4: Backups alone provide resilience

**Correction:** Backups support recovery only if they are secure, available, complete, and tested through restoration.

### Misconception 5: Cybersecurity and resilience have identical objectives

**Correction:** Cybersecurity primarily manages cyber risk. Resilience focuses on continuing outcomes despite disruption and adapting afterward.

## 16. Key Takeaways

1. Cybersecurity and resilience are related but not interchangeable.
2. Cybersecurity protects systems and manages cyber risks.
3. Resilience enables adaptation and continuation of essential outcomes during disruption.
4. Cybersecurity supports resilience through prevention, detection, response, and recovery.
5. Resilience assumes that controls can fail and prepares alternatives.
6. RTO, RPO, continuity planning, backup, and recovery are important resilience concepts.
7. OT and cyber-physical systems require resilience planning that considers safety and physical consequences.
8. Security and resilience objectives should be coordinated through risk-based decisions.
