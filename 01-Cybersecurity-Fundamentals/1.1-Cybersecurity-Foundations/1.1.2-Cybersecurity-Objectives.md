1.1.2 Cybersecurity Objectives

1. Overview

Cybersecurity objectives define the security outcomes an organization intends to achieve when protecting its information, technology, services, and interconnected environments.

They provide the basis for translating business requirements and risk assessments into security policies, architectural decisions, technical controls, operational procedures, and measurable results.

Cybersecurity objectives should be established according to the organization's mission, assets, threat environment, legal obligations, risk tolerance, and operational requirements.

2. Definition

Cybersecurity objectives are the intended security outcomes that guide the protection of systems, information, services, and operations against cyber risks.

They describe what must be protected and what level of protection or assurance is required. Security controls are selected and implemented to help achieve these objectives.

For example:

- Objective: Prevent unauthorized disclosure of customer information.
- Control: Apply access restrictions and encryption where appropriate.
- Evidence: Review access permissions and verify encryption configurations.

An objective describes the desired outcome; a control is a measure used to achieve it; evidence helps demonstrate whether the control is operating as intended.

3. Primary Security Objectives: The CIA Triad

The Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA) triad is a foundational model for information security.

3.1 Confidentiality

Definition: Ensuring that information is not disclosed to or accessed by unauthorized entities.

Confidentiality protects information from unauthorized viewing, exposure, or disclosure.

Common measures include:

- Identity and access management
- Least-privilege access
- Encryption at rest and in transit
- Data classification and handling rules
- Data-loss prevention where appropriate
- Secure management of credentials and cryptographic keys

Example: Only authorized healthcare personnel should be able to access a patient's medical records.

Failure scenario: A compromised account exposes confidential customer information.

Architectural consideration: Define trust boundaries, access policies, data flows, and appropriate protection mechanisms based on information sensitivity and risk.

3.2 Integrity

Definition: Protecting information and system states against unauthorized or improper modification, destruction, or manipulation.

Integrity supports the accuracy, completeness, and trustworthiness of data and system behavior.

Common measures include:

- Integrity verification using cryptographic hashes
- Digital signatures where appropriate
- Change management and approval workflows
- Input validation
- Configuration monitoring
- Audit logging and tamper protection
- Controlled access to critical system settings

Example: A financial transaction must not be altered without authorization.

Failure scenario: An attacker modifies industrial controller logic or manipulates sensor readings.

Architectural consideration: Identify critical data and configurations, define authorized change paths, and establish mechanisms to detect or prevent unauthorized modification.

3.3 Availability

Definition: Ensuring that information, systems, and services remain accessible and usable by authorized entities when required.

Availability depends on system reliability, capacity, recoverability, and resistance to disruption.

Common measures include:

- Redundancy and failover
- Tested backups and restoration procedures
- Capacity management
- Protection against denial-of-service attacks
- Monitoring and alerting
- Disaster recovery planning
- Resilient network and system design

Example: An emergency healthcare service must remain available when clinicians need it.

Failure scenario: Ransomware or a network outage prevents access to a critical application.

Architectural consideration: Define service availability requirements, acceptable downtime, recovery objectives, dependencies, and failure modes.

3.4 CIA Triad Summary

##Objective| Primary concern| Example failure
Confidentiality| Unauthorized disclosure| Sensitive records are exposed
Integrity| Unauthorized or improper modification| Transaction data is manipulated
Availability| Disruption of access or service| A critical application becomes unavailable #

The three objectives are complementary, but their relative priority varies by system and business context.

4. Additional Security Objectives

The CIA triad provides a foundation, but it does not cover every security requirement.

4.1 Authenticity

Authenticity provides confidence that an entity, message, or resource is what it claims to be.

Examples include verifying a user's identity, validating a server certificate, or checking the origin of a software update.

4.2 Accountability

Accountability means that actions and decisions can be attributed to responsible entities to an appropriate degree.

Common measures include individual accounts, audit trails, protected logs, and defined responsibilities.

4.3 Traceability

Traceability enables activities, changes, events, and decisions to be followed through relevant records.

It supports incident investigation, change verification, troubleshooting, and compliance evidence.

4.4 Non-repudiation

Non-repudiation refers to mechanisms that provide evidence supporting the origin, integrity, or occurrence of an action or communication, making denial more difficult.

Digital signatures can support certain non-repudiation requirements. However, technical mechanisms do not independently establish every legal or organizational conclusion.

4.5 Privacy

Privacy concerns the appropriate collection, use, sharing, retention, and disposal of personal information.

Privacy objectives may include data minimization, purpose limitation, appropriate transparency, and support for applicable individual rights.

Privacy and cybersecurity overlap, but they are not interchangeable. A system can be protected against unauthorized access while still processing personal information inappropriately.

4.6 Safety

Safety is particularly important in industrial environments, healthcare, transportation, and other systems that interact with the physical world.

Safety objectives seek to prevent unacceptable harm to people, property, or the environment.

A cybersecurity incident can create a safety hazard if it affects control logic, system availability, monitoring, or operator decision-making.

Cybersecurity controls must therefore be assessed for their possible effects on safe operations.

4.7 Resilience

Resilience concerns the ability to withstand disruption, adapt, recover, and continue delivering important outcomes.

Relevant measures include tested recovery plans, redundant capabilities, incident response, alternative operating procedures, and lessons learned.

Resilience complements availability but is broader than keeping a system continuously operational.

5. Security Objectives in Different Environments

The importance of each objective depends on the environment, assets, threats, and consequences of failure.

5.1 Enterprise IT

Important objectives include protecting business information, maintaining reliable applications, controlling user access, and preserving transaction integrity.

5.2 Cloud environments

Objectives include protecting identities and data, maintaining secure configurations, preserving workload integrity, and meeting availability requirements across service dependencies.

5.3 Internet of Things (IoT)

Objectives include authenticating devices, protecting telemetry, maintaining firmware integrity, securing device updates, and ensuring the availability of important device functions.

5.4 Operational Technology (OT)

In OT, the relative priority of confidentiality, integrity, and availability depends on the process and its risk context.

For some industrial systems, integrity and availability are especially critical because unauthorized changes or prolonged disruption can affect physical operations.

This does not mean confidentiality is unimportant. It means objectives must be selected according to process requirements, threat scenarios, and safety considerations.

5.5 Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS)

CPS security objectives may combine information protection with physical process integrity, dependable operation, safety, and recovery.

For example, a connected medical device may require trustworthy measurements, authorized configuration changes, reliable operation, and protection of patient information.

6. Translating Objectives into Security Requirements

Security objectives become useful when they can guide design, implementation, and assessment.

A practical sequence is:

1. Identify the business mission and important assets.
2. Identify relevant threats, vulnerabilities, and consequences.
3. Define the required security outcomes.
4. Convert those outcomes into specific requirements.
5. Select suitable technical and organizational controls.
6. Define how effectiveness will be measured.
7. Monitor results and revise requirements when risks change.

Example: Protecting a critical application

Element| Example
Business need| Authorized staff must access a critical application
Security objective| Preserve confidentiality and availability
Requirement| Enforce role-based access and define recovery targets
Controls| Access restrictions, monitoring, backups, and recovery procedures
Evidence| Access reviews, test results, monitoring records, and recovery exercises

The specific controls and thresholds must be selected using the application's risk assessment and operational requirements.

7. Measuring Achievement of Security Objectives

Objectives should be supported by appropriate evidence and measurable indicators.

Examples include:

- Percentage of privileged accounts reviewed within the required period
- Percentage of critical systems with verified recovery arrangements
- Time taken to detect and respond to defined incident types
- Percentage of critical assets with current ownership and classification records
- Number of unauthorized configuration changes detected
- Success rate of scheduled recovery exercises

Metrics should have clear definitions, data sources, owners, reporting intervals, and targets where appropriate.

A metric alone does not prove that a system is secure. Measurements must be interpreted alongside risk, control effectiveness, system context, and known limitations.

8. Common Misconceptions

Misconception 1: The CIA triad covers every security objective.

Correction: CIA is foundational, but systems may also require authenticity, accountability, privacy, safety, resilience, and other properties.

Misconception 2: Every system should prioritize all objectives equally.

Correction: Priorities depend on the mission, assets, threats, operational needs, and consequences of failure.

Misconception 3: Installing a security control proves an objective has been achieved.

Correction: Controls must be implemented correctly, tested, monitored, and evaluated against the intended outcome.

Misconception 4: Availability means a system can never experience downtime.

Correction: Availability requirements define acceptable service levels and recovery expectations. Absolute continuous availability may be infeasible or unjustified.

Misconception 5: Security objectives are exclusively technical.

Correction: Objectives also depend on governance, people, processes, suppliers, operational practices, and organizational decisions.

9. Architecture and Engineering Perspective

A cybersecurity architect translates security objectives into design principles, requirements, controls, and assurance activities.

Key responsibilities include:

- Identifying the security properties required for each asset and service
- Establishing trust boundaries and system dependencies
- Defining identity, access, and data-protection requirements
- Evaluating availability, integrity, and recovery needs
- Considering the interaction between cybersecurity and safety
- Mapping requirements to appropriate controls and standards
- Defining test methods and evidence of effectiveness
- Documenting trade-offs, assumptions, and residual risks

Architecture decisions should reflect the consequences of failure rather than applying identical controls to every system.

10. Governance and Risk Perspective

Security objectives help organizations make informed decisions about investment, risk acceptance, control selection, and performance.

Governance should establish:

- Who owns each security objective
- Which requirements are mandatory
- How risks and exceptions are approved
- What evidence is required
- How effectiveness is measured and reported
- When objectives must be reviewed

Objectives should align with organizational risk tolerance, legal and contractual requirements, business continuity needs, and operational realities.

11. Key Takeaways

1. Cybersecurity objectives define the outcomes that protection measures are intended to achieve.
2. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability form the foundational CIA triad.
3. Authenticity, accountability, traceability, non-repudiation, privacy, safety, and resilience may also be important.
4. Objectives should reflect the environment, threat model, business mission, and consequences of failure.
5. Security controls should be selected and tested against defined requirements.
6. Metrics and evidence support assessment but do not independently prove that a system is secure.
7. Cybersecurity architects translate objectives into design requirements, controls, and assurance activities.
