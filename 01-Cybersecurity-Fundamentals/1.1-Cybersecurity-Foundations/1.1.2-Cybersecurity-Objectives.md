# 1.1.2 — Cybersecurity Objectives

## 1. Overview

Cybersecurity objectives describe the security outcomes an organization wants to achieve when protecting its information, systems, services, and operations.

They help translate organizational needs and risks into security requirements, controls, and ways to evaluate whether protection is working.

Objectives depend on the organization’s mission, assets, threats, legal obligations, operational needs, and tolerance for risk. Not every system needs the same objectives or the same level of protection.

## 2. Objective, Requirement, Control, and Evidence

These terms are related, but they mean different things:

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | The desired security outcome | Prevent unauthorized disclosure of customer information |
| Requirement | A specific condition that must be met | Only approved roles may access customer records |
| Control | A measure used to meet a requirement | Role-based access restrictions |
| Evidence | Information used to assess whether a control is working | Access-review records and configuration checks |

An objective explains **what outcome is needed**. A control describes **a measure used to achieve it**. Evidence helps determine whether the measure is implemented and operating as intended.

## 3. The CIA Triad

Confidentiality, integrity, and availability—known as the **CIA triad**—are foundational information-security objectives.

### 3.1 Confidentiality

**Confidentiality** means protecting information from access or disclosure by unauthorized entities.

It may be supported by:

- Identity and access management
- Least-privilege access
- Data classification and handling rules
- Encryption where appropriate
- Secure management of credentials and cryptographic keys
- Monitoring for unauthorized access

**Example:** Only authorized healthcare personnel should be able to access a patient’s medical records.

**Possible failure:** A compromised account exposes confidential customer information.

### 3.2 Integrity

**Integrity** means protecting information and system states from unauthorized or improper modification, destruction, or manipulation.

It may be supported by:

- Approved change-management processes
- Access restrictions on important data and settings
- Input validation
- Configuration monitoring
- Integrity checks, such as cryptographic hashes, where appropriate
- Protected audit records

**Example:** A financial transaction must not be changed without authorization.

**Possible failure:** Unauthorized changes to a system’s configuration produce inaccurate results or unsafe operation.

### 3.3 Availability

**Availability** means ensuring that information, systems, and services are accessible and usable by authorized entities when required.

It may be supported by:

- Reliable system design
- Capacity planning
- Redundancy and failover where appropriate
- Tested backups and restoration procedures
- Monitoring and alerting
- Incident response and recovery planning

**Example:** Clinicians can access a critical healthcare application when they need it.

**Possible failure:** A system outage prevents authorized users from accessing an essential service.

Availability does not necessarily mean zero downtime. Requirements should define acceptable service levels and recovery expectations for the specific system.

### 3.4 CIA Triad Summary

| Objective | Main concern | Example failure |
|---|---|---|
| Confidentiality | Unauthorized access or disclosure | Sensitive records are exposed |
| Integrity | Unauthorized or improper modification | Transaction information is altered |
| Availability | Disruption of access or service | A critical application becomes unavailable |

The relative importance of each objective varies with the system and its consequences of failure.

## 4. Other Security Properties and Related Objectives

The CIA triad provides a foundation, but systems may need additional security properties.

### 4.1 Authenticity

**Authenticity** provides confidence that an entity, message, or resource is genuine.

Examples include verifying a user’s identity, checking a server certificate, or confirming the source of a software update.

### 4.2 Accountability

**Accountability** means that actions can be attributed to responsible entities to an appropriate degree.

Individual accounts, defined responsibilities, and protected audit records can support accountability.

### 4.3 Traceability

**Traceability** makes it possible to follow relevant activities, changes, events, or decisions through records. It can support investigations, troubleshooting, and reviews.

### 4.4 Non-repudiation

**Non-repudiation** uses evidence to support the origin, integrity, or occurrence of an action or communication, making it more difficult to deny.

For example, a digital signature can provide evidence about the origin and integrity of signed data. Technical evidence alone does not determine every legal or organizational conclusion.

### 4.5 Privacy

**Privacy** concerns the appropriate collection, use, sharing, retention, and disposal of personal information.

Cybersecurity and privacy overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Information may be protected from unauthorized access while still being collected or used inappropriately.

### 4.6 Safety

**Safety** concerns preventing unacceptable harm to people, property, or the environment. It is especially important when digital systems interact with physical processes.

Cybersecurity decisions should consider whether a control or incident could affect safe operation.

### 4.7 Resilience

**Resilience** is the ability to withstand disruption, adapt, recover, and continue delivering important outcomes. It complements availability but is broader than keeping a system continuously operational.

## 5. Objectives Depend on the Environment

Security priorities should reflect the purpose of the system, the information it handles, its threats, and the consequences of failure.

### Enterprise IT

Objectives may include protecting business information, controlling user access, preserving transaction integrity, and maintaining important applications.

### Cloud environments

Objectives may include protecting identities and data, maintaining secure configurations, preserving workload integrity, and meeting availability requirements across service dependencies.

### Internet of Things

Objectives may include authenticating devices, protecting telemetry, maintaining firmware integrity, securing update processes, and keeping essential device functions available.

### Operational technology

In operational technology, the priority given to confidentiality, integrity, and availability depends on the process and its risk context. Integrity and availability may be especially important where unauthorized changes or disruption could affect physical operations. Safety must also be considered.

Confidentiality remains relevant; priorities should be based on the actual requirements and consequences of failure.

### Cyber-physical systems

Cyber-physical systems may need objectives that combine information protection with dependable operation, physical-process integrity, safety, and recovery.

For example, a connected medical device may need trustworthy measurements, authorized configuration changes, reliable operation, and protection of patient information.

## 6. Turning Objectives into Requirements and Controls

A practical sequence for applying security objectives is:

1. Identify the system’s mission, users, and important assets.
2. Identify relevant threats, vulnerabilities, dependencies, and consequences.
3. Define the security outcomes required for the system.
4. Translate each outcome into clear and testable requirements.
5. Select suitable technical and organizational controls.
6. Decide what evidence will help assess whether the controls are effective.
7. Review the objectives and requirements when risks or system needs change.

**Example: Protecting a critical application**

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Business need | Authorized staff need access to a critical application |
| Security objective | Protect sensitive information and maintain required availability |
| Requirement | Limit access to approved roles and define recovery expectations |
| Controls | Access restrictions, monitoring, backups, and recovery procedures |
| Evidence | Access reviews, monitoring records, and recovery-test results |

The specific controls and targets should be selected based on the system’s risk assessment and operational requirements.

## 7. Measuring Progress

Organizations can use indicators and evidence to evaluate progress toward security objectives.

Examples include:

- Percentage of privileged accounts reviewed within the required period
- Percentage of critical systems with tested recovery arrangements
- Time taken to detect and respond to defined incident types
- Percentage of critical assets with current ownership records
- Number of unauthorized configuration changes identified
- Results of scheduled recovery exercises

Useful measures should have clear definitions, data sources, owners, reporting intervals, and targets where appropriate.

A metric does not prove that a system is secure by itself. Results must be considered alongside risk, control effectiveness, system context, and limitations in the available evidence.

## 8. Common Misconceptions

### Misconception 1: The CIA triad covers every security objective

**Correction:** CIA is foundational, but a system may also need authenticity, accountability, privacy, safety, resilience, or other properties.

### Misconception 2: Every system should prioritize all objectives equally

**Correction:** Priorities depend on the system’s mission, assets, threats, operational requirements, and consequences of failure.

### Misconception 3: Installing a control proves that an objective has been achieved

**Correction:** Controls need to be implemented correctly and assessed to determine whether they support the intended outcome.

### Misconception 4: Availability means a system can never be unavailable

**Correction:** Availability requirements define acceptable service levels and recovery expectations. Continuous availability may not be feasible or necessary for every system.

### Misconception 5: Security objectives are only technical

**Correction:** Achieving security objectives can depend on governance, people, processes, suppliers, operational practices, and technology.

## 9. Key Takeaways

1. Cybersecurity objectives describe the outcomes that protection measures are intended to achieve.
2. Confidentiality, integrity, and availability form a foundational model.
3. Authenticity, accountability, traceability, privacy, safety, resilience, and other properties may also matter.
4. Objectives and priorities should reflect the system’s context and risks.
5. Requirements and controls should be selected to support defined objectives.
6. Evidence and metrics help assess progress, but do not independently prove that a system is secure.
