# Domain 14: Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC)

## Overview

Governance, Risk and Compliance (GRC) is a structured approach used by organizations to direct cybersecurity activities, manage risk, meet legal and regulatory obligations, and align security with business objectives.

GRC connects:

- Business strategy
- Cybersecurity governance
- Enterprise risk management
- Regulatory compliance
- Security policies
- Internal controls
- Audit and assurance
- Third-party risk
- Security performance
- Continuous improvement

A mature GRC program ensures that cybersecurity is not treated only as a technical function. It becomes an integrated business capability supported by leadership, processes, technology, accountability, and measurable outcomes.

For digital products, IoT environments, industrial systems, and cyber-physical systems, GRC must consider:

- Confidentiality
- Integrity
- Availability
- Safety
- Reliability
- Resilience
- Privacy
- Regulatory obligations
- Product security
- Supply-chain risk
- Business continuity

## 1. Understanding Governance, Risk and Compliance

### Governance

Governance defines how an organization is directed, controlled, and held accountable.

Cybersecurity governance establishes:

- Security objectives
- Leadership responsibilities
- Decision-making authority
- Security policies
- Risk ownership
- Accountability
- Reporting structures
- Performance expectations

### Risk

Risk management identifies and evaluates uncertainty that may affect business objectives.

Cybersecurity risk may result from:

- Threats
- Vulnerabilities
- Poor architecture
- Weak processes
- Third-party dependencies
- Regulatory changes
- Human error
- Technology failure
- Operational disruption

### Compliance

Compliance is the process of meeting applicable:

- Laws
- Regulations
- Standards
- Contracts
- Internal policies
- Customer requirements
- Industry obligations

Compliance demonstrates that required controls and obligations are being addressed. Compliance alone does not guarantee complete security.

### Three Lines Model

A commonly used risk governance model includes:

#### First Line: Business and Operations

Responsible for:

- Owning business processes
- Operating security controls
- Managing day-to-day risks
- Following approved policies
- Reporting issues

#### Second Line: Risk, Compliance and Security Oversight

Responsible for:

- Defining frameworks

#### Third Line: Internal Audit

Responsible for:

- Independent assurance
- Evaluating governance
- Assessing risk management
- Reviewing control effectiveness
- Reporting audit findings

The exact organizational structure may differ between organizations.

- Exposure
- Business dependency
- Data sensitivity
- Regulatory impact
- Safety impact
- Recovery capability

### Qualitative Risk Assessment

Uses descriptive ratings such as:

- Medium

## 8. Risk Treatment

Organizations generally use the following risk treatment options.

### Risk Mitigation

Implement controls to reduce likelihood or impact.

Examples:

- Network segmentation
- Multi-factor authentication
- Encryption
- Monitoring
- Backup
- Secure configuration
- Security awareness
- Application security controls

### Risk Avoidance

Stop or change an activity that creates unacceptable risk.

Examples:

- Discontinuing an insecure service
- Removing unnecessary internet exposure
- Avoiding unsupported technology
- Not deploying a high-risk feature

### Risk Transfer

Share some financial or operational impact with another party.

Examples:

- Cyber insurance
- Contractual agreements
- Outsourcing
- Service-level agreements

Risk transfer does not eliminate the underlying cybersecurity responsibility.

### Risk Acceptance

Formally acknowledge and accept a defined level of risk.

Risk acceptance should include:

- Documented justification
- Risk owner approval
- Business impact
- Compensating controls
- Review date
- Expiry date where appropriate

## 9. Inherent Risk and Residual Risk

### Inherent Risk

Inherent risk is the level of risk before considering existing controls.

### Residual Risk

Residual risk is the remaining risk after controls and risk treatment have been applied.

### Example

A publicly exposed application may have high inherent risk because it is accessible to external attackers.

After implementing:

- Strong authentication
- Secure configuration
- Network restrictions
- Monitoring
- Vulnerability management
- Incident response procedures

The residual risk may be reduced, but it may not become zero.

## 10. Risk Register

A risk register is a structured record of identified risks.

### Typical Risk Register Fields

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| Risk ID | Unique identifier |
| Risk title | Short description |
| Risk statement | Description of the risk event |
| Asset or process | Affected business asset or process |
| Threat | Potential cause of harm |
| Vulnerability | Weakness that may be exploited |
| Likelihood | Probability or likelihood rating |
| Impact | Business impact rating |
| Inherent risk | Risk before controls |
| Existing controls | Current safeguards |
| Residual risk | Remaining risk |
| Risk owner | Person accountable for the risk |
| Treatment plan | Planned action |
| Target date | Expected completion date |
| Status | Open, in progress, accepted, or closed |
| Review date | Next review date |

### Risk Statement Format

A useful risk statement may follow this structure:

Because of [threat or weakness], [event] may occur, resulting in [business impact].

### Protect

Implement safeguards such as:

- Access control

- Data security
- Protective technology
- Security procedures

### Detect

- Monitoring
- Detection processes
- Anomaly identification
- Security event analysis

- Response planning
- Communications
- Analysis
- Mitigation
- Improvements

### Recover

## 17. Findings and Corrective Action

Audit or assessment findings should be managed through a formal corrective action process.

### Finding Management Lifecycle

1. Identify finding
2. Validate finding
3. Assign owner
4. Determine root cause
5. Define corrective action
6. Set target date
7. Track progress
8. Validate completion
9. Record evidence
10. Close finding

### Root Cause Analysis

Root cause analysis asks why the issue occurred rather than only correcting the immediate symptom.

Possible root causes include:

- Missing policy
- Inadequate training
- Poor process design
- Lack of ownership
- Insufficient monitoring
- Inadequate testing
- Resource limitations
- Weak change management
- Incomplete requirements
